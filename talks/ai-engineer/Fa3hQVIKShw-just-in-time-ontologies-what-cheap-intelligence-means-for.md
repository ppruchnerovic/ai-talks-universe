## Description

A pipeline stripped the minus signs from loan amounts and turned a net $784,000 into $5.3 million of debt, with multiple models agreeing on the wrong result. Hrishi Olickel uses failures like this to explain why transforming data requires more than a universal schema or a confident agent. A customer means something different to billing, support and compliance. Useful structure depends on the source, the storage constraints, the decision being made and the assumptions a model brings to the task. His alternative is a just in time ontology: build the transformation when the question is known, and preserve its context and evidence for reuse.

An Ohio campaign finance entity resolution project provides the working example. Olickel describes separating models that design a pipeline, models that perform its individual jobs and models that optimize the resulting system. Definitions, held out cases and disagreement checks matter as much as model selection. Comparing different model families can concentrate review on their disagreements, but shared assumptions can still produce the same wrong answer. The optimization loop needs a stable execution surface, bounded budgets, reusable methods and a defined way to fail. Small pilots establish a baseline; independently checked work segments make revisions easier to inspect and revert. Only then can the pipeline trade among cost, speed and accuracy without losing the evidence behind its answers.
