ABSTRACT
Cedric Hurst takes us inside his experience building and running an autonomous AI agent, Evie, using OpenClaw. Rather than simply answering prompts or writing code, the agent maintains persistent memory, connects to email, Slack, calendars and other services, processes text, images and voice, creates its own skills, and proactively finds ways to help. Hurst shows how this changes the relationship from using an AI tool to working with an AI “partner” that can operate continuously across projects and everyday life.

But greater autonomy also brings greater responsibility. Hurst emphasizes that agents should be treated as potentially powerful—and potentially dangerous—systems: run them with minimal privileges, protect credentials, quarantine untrusted inputs, carefully control external communication, and don't blindly install third-party skills. His conclusion is pragmatic rather than utopian: keep experimenting, build your own guardrails, and let agents evolve through real-world use while keeping humans in control of sensitive decisions.
